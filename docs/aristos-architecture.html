The one rule: math judges, LLM writes

Every BUY, HOLD or SELL you see comes out of a fixed, written-down recipe — not out of a conversation with an AI. A company is scored against its peers on a handful of measurable things (how cheap it is, how profitable, how its price has been moving, and so on), the scores are added up, and the ranking decides the verdict. That part never changes its mind and never gets talked into a different answer.

The AI's only job is to explain the verdict the math already reached — never to issue one, never to override one, and never to average several lenses' verdicts into a single "net view." If you tick more than one lens for the same company, you get more than one independent verdict, reported side by side.

This was tested, not assumed: an earlier version let the AI reach the verdict directly, and a controlled check found it gave a different answer on a rerun of the exact same inputs. That version still exists for comparison (it is called "second_opinion mode" in the code and is tucked away behind a validation toggle) but it is not what drives the product.

The lenses — what each one asks

A "lens" is one scoring recipe, saved and versioned so it can never quietly change underneath a past result. Ten are visible in the app today: seven score ordinary companies ("stocks") and three score exchange-traded funds ("ETFs"). The app shows stock lenses by default and switches to the ETF ones only when you flip "Asset type" to ETFs in the sidebar.

LensWhat it asks
Value + Momentum (the flagship) The same as Magic Formula RAW (companies worth at least $5bn, not banks, insurers or utilities), but only for companies earning at least 12% on their capital.
Defensive Income Steady income with the trend intact: a yield of at least 1.5%, paid from cash (no more than 80% of free cash flow) and raised for 10 years, from a company worth at least $5bn with debt no bigger than its market value and a share price not down more than 10%; the calmest share ranks best.
Cyclical Income Income that survives the cycle: a yield of at least 1.5%, paid from cash (no more than 80% of free cash flow), not cut in five years, with debt no bigger than the company's market value, from a company worth at least $5bn (not a bank or insurer).
Earnings Power Value Cheap for the profit it already makes: what the business is worth if today's operating profit simply continues with no growth, compared with what the market charges for it, for companies worth at least $5bn (not banks or insurers).
Financials Banks and insurers on their own terms: return on equity against price-to-book, for financial companies worth at least $5bn.
Forensic (a check, never a vote — see below) Are the profits real: cash behind the earnings, a safe balance sheet, and improving health checks, for companies worth at least $5bn (not banks or insurers).
Growth Growing fast at a fair price: sales up at least 10% a year, earning at least 12% on its capital, and not overpaying for that growth (its price no more than twice its growth rate), for companies worth at least $5bn (not banks or insurers).
Magic Formula RAW Cheap and good: high profit on the money invested, a low price for that profit, and a rising share, for companies worth at least $5bn (not banks, insurers or utilities).
Quality A strong, durable business: high gross profit on its assets, a decent return on capital even in its worst recent year, and little debt against its operating profit, for companies worth at least $5bn (not banks or insurers).

These two tables are generated from each lens's own caption in the app (the sentence under its tick box), so they cannot drift from what the app says. python -m scripts.build_architecture_doc rewrites them.

Every ticked lens ranks a company only against the other names it is able to rank — so "3rd of 40" under one lens and "3rd of 12" under another are both correct; the cohort sizes are allowed to differ. The exact formula behind each lens is in docs/CALCULATIONS.md.

Votes versus marks: Forensic and the valuation band

Every lens you tick for a company gets an equal vote — BUY, HOLD or SELL — and the app simply counts them ("BUY on 2 of 3 votes"). Nothing is weighted, nothing is combined into a single number.

Forensic doesn't vote — it marks

Forensic looks for accounting warning signs, but a clean-looking balance sheet is not a reason to buy something and a doubtful one is not automatically a reason to sell. So its reading is reported in its own words — clean, no concern, or doubted — sitting beside the vote, never replacing or outvoting it.

The valuation band is also a mark, not a vote

This answers a different question from any lens: "is this company cheap or expensive against its own five-year history?" — shown as a percentile (e.g. "cheaper than 92% of its own history"). It never changes a verdict; it is a caution a reader sees alongside it, always shown, whichever way it points.

Cohorts and peer groups

To rank a company, the app needs to know who its peers are. That comes from a market index — a locally-built table of roughly 25,000 listed companies across 18 stock-exchange regions — kept up to date by its own refresh job, separate from any one report.

  • The peer ladder — for one company, the app pulls a same-industry peer group sized between 12 and 40 names, widening or narrowing by company size so a $2bn company isn't compared against $200bn ones.
  • Cohorts — fixed, versioned groups of 20–60 companies in one industry (e.g. "Industrials - Grid & Electrical Machinery"), built from the same index and frozen so a backtest or track-record claim is always measured against the same membership list, not a list that quietly drifted.
  • The small-company view — every lens has a minimum company size it is actually tested against (usually $5bn). A smaller company isn't silently excluded: it is automatically ranked against its own size band instead, and every vote for it carries a visible caveat that it sits outside the lens's tested range. There is no tick box for this any more — the app detects it and switches automatically.

The Analyse tab: one company, or a list

There is one place to run anything: the Analyse tab. A switch at the top — "Company" or "Cohort / list" — decides what you're looking at; there is no separate page for a single company any more.

  • Company mode — type or pick one ticker. The app shows every ticked lens's verdict for it, its peer table, its valuation band, its price and cash position, and (if asked for) the plain-English summary and the council's narration.
  • Cohort / list mode — paste or pick a list of tickers and the app ranks every name under every ticked lens at once, showing a results table with one column per lens. Under the results, "Open a company page" loads the name you pick into Company mode with the same lenses ticked, and does not run anything. When you press Run there, the app runs a fresh check of that company against its own industry peers — not against your list.

The Scoreboard tab, alongside Analyse, is a read-only log of past ranking runs — one row per ranked name per graded run — kept so a claim about a lens's track record can be checked later against what it actually said at the time.

Track-record badges

Next to a voting lens's verdict you may see a small badge. It answers one question: over the last ten years, did buying this lens's picks in this industry actually beat a random pick from the same group, after costs? It is a statement about the past, never a promise about the future.

BadgeMeaning
proven here Beat its benchmark in this cohort by enough, often enough, for long enough, that fewer than 1 in 20 random stock-pickers matched it.
promising here Beat its benchmark in this cohort by a real margin most years, and did better than most random stock-pickers - not yet enough history or separation from luck to call it proven.
worked against you here In this cohort, picking names at random would have matched or beaten this lens at least 9 times in 10.
no edge shown here In this cohort, this lens's record does not clear the bar, and does no better than picking names at random.
untested here There is not yet enough measured history for this cohort and this lens to say anything.

These are the five labels the app shows, in the app's own words. A check lens (Forensic) marks and does not vote, so it carries no badge. A lens that did not apply to the company shows its reason instead of a badge.

The owner's standing ruling (2026-10-02): a backtest result is cyclical and is not the deciding factor for loosening a lens's own size floor — the small-company view above handles smaller companies instead.

The council and the plain-English summary

Two features sit on top of the deterministic verdict, both optional, both off by default, and both cost real API credits to run:

  • Plain-English summary — one short, model-written paragraph restating what the numbers already show, in plain words.
  • Council opinion — four AI "specialists" (fundamental, technical, sentiment, risk) each write a short independent take, an adversarial "critic" tries to poke holes in the case, and the result is narrated for a reader.
Neither one ever changes, votes on, or overrides the verdict. The agreement table above the narration — "BUY on 2 of 3 votes," say — stays the verdict of record no matter what the council writes underneath it. Several deterministic checks run after the fact specifically to catch and flag the AI drifting from that rule (for example, if it ever tried to credit a non-voting check lens with casting a BUY/HOLD/SELL).

Gap Ledger — a separate app

Gap Ledger is a different tool that happens to live in the same codebase. It answers a completely different question — "which US stocks are moving before the market opens this morning, and why?" — not "is this a good business at this price?" It keeps its own daily shortlist, its own control group, and its own grading after the close.

No part of the council links to it, and it does not read anything from the council. It has its own separate screen (started from the command line, not a button) and its own separate, read-only web page.

Where the numbers come from, and their limits

  • Prices, fundamentals and dividend history mainly come from Yahoo Finance, with EODHD used for the market index and some fundamentals lookups.
  • Recent news on the company page ("Recent news: EODHD news") comes from EODHD, with Yahoo Finance's news as the fallback when EODHD has none.
  • News sentiment — only used by the council's sentiment specialist, an optional paid feature — reads Finnhub, which on the plan this app uses covers US-listed companies only; for other companies it falls back to the same EODHD / Yahoo news.
  • Everything is cached for a day so repeated runs don't needlessly re-fetch the same figures.
  • Missing data is never guessed. If a number genuinely isn't available, the app says so plainly ("not evaluated — insufficient history") rather than quietly treating the gap as a zero, a fail, or a pass. The honest answer is always preferred to a confident-looking wrong one.
